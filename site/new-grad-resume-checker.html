New grad guide

New grad resume checker: prove you can do the job without inventing experience.

You do not need years of full-time experience to have evidence. You need to show scope, tools, and outcomes from the experience you do have. Here is how to build an entry-level resume that a parser and a recruiter can both read.

Why new grad resumes get filtered

Entry-level resumes often read like a list of courses and soft adjectives: hardworking, detail-oriented, passionate about technology. None of that tells a recruiter what you can do. If the posting asks for Python, SQL, and a portfolio, the resume needs evidence of those things, not a claim that you are eager to learn them.

The other common failure is structural. A two-column template, a photo, or a skills chart can look polished and still parse badly. A new grad resume does not get an exemption from the same parser rules as anyone else's.

Build an evidence inventory

List the work you actually did: projects, internships, part-time jobs, volunteering, coursework, and certifications. For each one, write the scope, the tools, and a result. Then translate it into the target role's language.

What you didEvidence to showTarget-role language
Built a course-planning app with three classmatesStack, users, performanceBuilt a Flask and PostgreSQL app with a three-person team; cut page load from 2.1s to 1.2s for 200 test users
Interned at a small marketing agencyDeliverables, tools, resultProduced 12 social campaigns in Canva and Hootsuite; increased engagement 18% over eight weeks
Worked part-time in retailVolume, reliability, trainingTrained five new hires and handled 60+ customers per shift while maintaining 99% checkout accuracy

Use honest scope when you do not have a metric. “Team of four,” “12-table schema,” “200 test users,” and “three-week deadline” are all evidence. Do not invent numbers to fill the column.

Pick one target role and extract its terms

Choose one entry-level role, not five. Pull the top eight to twelve must-haves from a real posting. The keyword extractor and the role guides can help. Then mark which requirements you can support with a project, internship, or part-time example.

If a posting asks for three years of experience and you have none, that is a targeting problem, not a resume wording problem. Apply to roles whose core requirements you mostly meet, and use the resume to make the match obvious.

Write a summary that leads with evidence

Use a simple formula: target role + degree or graduation date + two relevant skills + one result. Compare:

Before

Motivated computer science graduate seeking an entry-level software engineering role.

After

Computer science graduate (May 2026) with project experience in Python, Flask, and PostgreSQL. Built a 200-user course-planning app with a three-person team and cut query time by 40%.

The second version names the target, the stack, and a concrete result. It does not claim professional experience you do not have.

Put projects and internships in the right places

Use a standard order: Education, Skills, Experience, Projects. If your projects are stronger than your work history, put a Projects section directly under Education. Label internships and part-time jobs accurately; do not call coursework “professional experience.”

For each project, include the problem, your role, the stack, and the result. A link to a live demo or repository is useful if it is working and you are proud of it. Do not include a link you have not checked.

Keep the format boring

One column. Standard headings. Real selectable text. Month-and-year dates. No photo, no skill bars, no tables or text boxes holding key content. Keep it to one page unless you have a genuine reason to go longer.

Use a plain PDF export and run the copy-paste test: select the text, paste it into a plain editor, and see whether the resume still reads. If it does not, an ATS may see the same thing.

Scan against an entry-level posting

Paste the resume and a real entry-level posting into the scanner. Fix structure warnings first, add three to five missing terms that are true, and rewrite two bullets with scope and a result. Re-scan. Stop when the remaining gaps are things you cannot claim.

Do not scan against a senior role to chase a higher score. The score is only meaningful against a job you can actually pursue.

Common new grad mistakes

  • Listing courses instead of evidence. A course name is not a result; a project is.
  • Using soft adjectives as skills. “Hardworking” and “team player” are not searchable tools.
  • Hiding projects below unrelated jobs. Lead with the evidence that matches the target role.
  • Using a decorative template. Columns, photos, and skill bars can break parsing.
  • Applying to roles that require experience you do not have. Target entry-level roles whose must-haves you mostly meet.
  • Chasing a perfect score. Past the high 80s, you are usually optimizing for the checker, not the hiring manager.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a new grad resume be?

One page is the right default. You are selecting the most relevant projects, internships, and part-time work, not cataloguing every course. If you have several years of relevant internships and publications, two pages can be justified, but one page is easier for a recruiter to skim.

Do I need an internship to have a good new grad resume?

No. Projects, part-time jobs, volunteering, coursework, and certifications can all show evidence. What matters is that each entry names the scope, the tools, and a result. An internship is useful, but it is not the only proof that you can do the work.

Should I include my GPA?

Include it if it is strong for your field, if the posting requires it, or if you are applying to a graduate program. Otherwise, leave it out and use the space for projects and evidence. A GPA without context rarely helps a recruiter decide.

Can I list coursework as experience?

Put coursework under Education or a Projects section, not under professional experience. Do not imply you were employed when you were not. A capstone project can still be strong evidence if you describe the scope, your role, and the result.

What if my projects have no metrics?

Use honest scope instead: team size, users, dataset size, number of features, runtime, grade, or time saved. “Built a 12-table SQL schema for a course project” is more useful than “worked with databases.” Do not invent numbers.

Should I include a photo or a skill-bar chart?

No. Photos and graphical skill bars add little for an ATS and can distract a recruiter. Use plain text, standard headings, and a simple skills list. Keep the design clean rather than decorative.

How do I handle little or no work experience?

Lead with projects, relevant coursework, and the skills you can defend. Use part-time or volunteer work to show reliability, customer handling, and teamwork. Apply to entry-level roles whose must-haves you mostly meet, and build direct evidence through a project or internship where you can.
